What to Show When a Directory Listing Has No Photo

Open almost any local directory built from Google Maps data and scroll a city page for a minute. Some cards have a bright storefront photo, some have a blurry shot of a parking lot, and a fair number have nothing at all. That last group is where a directory starts to look unfinished, and it is usually the group the owner thinks about last.

This note is about designing for the listing with no photo. It covers placeholders, when to drop the image slot entirely, how to collect photos from the businesses themselves and why copying images from a Maps listing is a habit to avoid.

Why so many listings arrive without a usable photo

A Google Maps export from Outscraper, Apify or any scraper often includes a photo field, but the field tells you less than it seems. Many photos on a Maps profile were uploaded by customers rather than the business. Small trades like mobile mechanics or solo cleaners may have no photos at all because they don't have a storefront to photograph.

There is also a rights question. Photos on a business profile belong to whoever took them, and Google's own developer documentation sets out attribution rules for showing place photos through its APIs. A directory that hotlinks or copies those images into its own pages takes on a risk it doesn't need. The safer starting point is to treat every listing as photo-less until the business gives you an image or you have a source you are allowed to use.

So the design question changes: what should a card look like with no photo? A real photo then becomes a bonus.

Design the card so it works with no image

If the layout depends on a photo, every missing image leaves a hole. A card built around text first holds up much better.

On a phone, a simple pattern works for most niches: the business name in large type, the town underneath, one line of useful detail such as hours or a service, then the action buttons. A photo, when there is one, sits on the left as a small square or across the top as a short banner. When there isn't one, the text simply moves up and the card gets shorter.

A few details make that switch look deliberate:

  • Keep the card padding the same with or without an image so the column doesn't jump.
  • Avoid fixed card heights. Let photo-less cards be shorter rather than padding them out.
  • Put the photo after the text in the HTML so screen readers reach the name first.
  • Test a page where only one listing in ten has a photo, because that is a common real case.

Placeholders that help instead of hiding the gap

Sometimes you do want a consistent visual block on every card, for example in a grid view on desktop. Then you need a placeholder, and the choice matters.

Generic stock photos are the weakest option. A smiling plumber who doesn't work at the listed company is misleading, and visitors notice when three different plumbers share the same face. Grey boxes with a broken image icon look like an error.

Better placeholders carry information. An icon for the category, drawn in your brand color, tells someone what kind of business it is at a glance. A tile with the business initials on a soft background works well for professional services where photos rarely add much. For a food directory, a neutral pattern with the cuisine label can stand in until a real photo arrives.

Whatever you choose, keep it obviously a placeholder. Nobody should mistake it for a picture of the actual business.

When to drop the image slot entirely

Some niches don't need photos on cards. Accountants, insurance agents and lawyers rarely win a visitor's choice because of a storefront photo. In those directories the space is better spent on a service line, an opening hours badge or a clear phone button.

Other niches lean heavily on images. Restaurants, salons, campgrounds and wedding venues are compared partly by how they look, so a card without a photo there is a real disadvantage. For those, it's worth making photo collection part of how you run the directory rather than an afterthought.

A quick way to decide: look at your current data and count how many listings have a photo you could legally use. If it's a small minority, design for text-first cards and let photos be an upgrade on the listing page.

Getting photos from the businesses themselves

The cleanest source of a listing photo is the business owner. They know which picture represents them, and they can give you permission to use it.

You can ask in a few low-effort ways. Add a line to the listing page inviting the owner to send a photo through your contact form. Mention it when a business writes in to correct its details. If you offer featured placements, include a photo slot and ask for the image as part of setup.

When a photo comes in, keep a short record of who sent it and when. If the business later asks you to remove it, you can find it quickly.

Questions about listings without photos

Can I use the photos from a Google Maps listing on my directory?

It's risky. Many of those photos were uploaded by customers, and the business may not own them either. Google also publishes attribution requirements for photos served through its APIs. Ask the business for an image or use a placeholder you made yourself.

Should every listing in a category use the same placeholder?

Usually yes. A single category icon or initials tile keeps the page calm and makes the real photos stand out. Mixing several stock images across listings tends to look cluttered and can mislead visitors.

Do photos on cards slow a directory down?

They can if the files are large. Compress images, set width and height attributes and lazy-load images below the first screen. A text-first card with an optional small photo is lighter than a photo-first grid.
