How to Write Headings for City and Category Pages

1 Click Directory Builder · November 24, 2026 · 5 min read
In this article

  2. Let the count and the area do the describing
  3. Write category headings as plain answers
  4. Keep headings consistent across the template
  5. Words to drop from directory headings

The heading at the top of a city or category page does more work than almost any other line on a directory. It is the first thing a visitor reads after the page loads, it often becomes the page title in search results, and it decides whether someone keeps scrolling or taps back. Yet on most directory templates it gets filled with a pattern like "Plumbers in Springfield" and never looked at again.

That pattern is fine as a starting point. The trouble starts when the same formula runs across three hundred pages, some with forty listings and some with two, and every heading promises the same thing. This note looks at how to write headings and the short lines around them so each page says plainly what it holds.

Start with what the visitor typed

People arrive on city and category pages from a search, and the words they used are usually simple. They type a trade and a place. A heading that repeats those words back confirms they landed in the right spot, which matters more than sounding clever.

So the base heading should name the service and the area in the order people say them out loud. "Dog groomers in Tacoma" reads naturally. "Tacoma's dog grooming directory" puts the place first and adds a word nobody searched for. Keep the trade word close to what locals actually call it. If your export uses a Google category like "Pet groomer" but your visitors say "dog groomer", write the heading in their words and keep the Google term for your filters.

Avoid stacking qualifiers into the heading. "Best top-rated affordable dog groomers in Tacoma, WA" tries to match every possible search and ends up sounding like an ad. One trade and one place is enough for the heading. The detail can go in the line under it.

Let the count and the area do the describing

The line directly under the heading is where a page earns its keep. Instead of a generic welcome, use it to state facts the visitor can check: how many listings the page holds, what area it covers and what they can filter by.

A line like "23 groomers across Tacoma and nearby Fife, sorted by distance from the city center" tells the visitor what they are looking at. It also changes from page to page on its own, because the count and the area are different everywhere. That makes your city pages read less like copies of each other without you writing three hundred custom intros.

Be careful with counts on small pages. "2 groomers in Ruston" is honest, and honesty helps here. If a page has very few listings, a short note pointing to the nearest larger city page is more useful than padding the heading to sound bigger.

Write category headings as plain answers

Category pages cover a type of business across your whole area, and their headings can carry a little more explanation. A visitor on "Mobile groomers" may not know whether that means a van that comes to the house or a groomer who travels to a salon. The heading can stay short, and a one-sentence subheading can settle the question: "Groomers who come to your home in a van or trailer."

This is also the place to be clear about scope. If your mobile groomer category only includes businesses whose Google listing says they offer mobile service, say that the list comes from the business's own listing. Visitors trust a short, specific claim far more than a broad one.

Keep headings consistent across the template

Visitors move between pages. Someone checks the Tacoma page, then the Fife page, then the mobile groomers category. If the heading style changes every time, the site feels stitched together.

Pick a small set of patterns and stick to them:

  • City pages: trade plus city, with a count line underneath
  • County pages: trade plus county name, with the list of cities below
  • Category pages: the category name in plain words, with a one-sentence definition
  • Guide pages: a question or a task, written the way a visitor would ask it

Write these patterns down before you build. Changing a template later is easy on a static site, but rewriting dozens of hand-edited headings is tedious work that nobody enjoys.

Words to drop from directory headings

Some words creep into directory headings because they feel like they help with search. Most of them make the page less believable.

"Best" is the obvious one. Unless you explain how you chose the order, such as by Google rating with a minimum number of reviews, a heading that says "best" claims something you cannot back up. "Top" has the same problem. "Trusted", "verified" and "certified" are worse, because they imply you checked something about each business. If you did not call each groomer and confirm a license or insurance, leave those words out.

"Near me" is another trap. It reads oddly on a page about a fixed city, and the person reading already knows where they are. Use the city name instead.

Questions about directory page headings

Should the page title tag match the visible heading?

They can be close without being identical. The title tag can add the state or the site name, since it shows in search results where people compare several sites. The visible heading can be shorter because the visitor is already on your page.

How do I handle cities with the same name in different states?

Add the state abbreviation to the heading on those pages, and on any page where your directory covers more than one state. "Dog groomers in Springfield, IL" removes the guesswork for visitors and for search engines.

Is it worth writing custom headings for my biggest cities?

Yes, for a handful of pages. The pages that get the most visits are worth a hand-written subheading that mentions a neighborhood or a detail only a local would know.
