How to Design Listing Cards That Work on a Phone

1 Click Directory Builder · November 23, 2026 · 5 min read
In this article

The listing card is the unit a directory visitor actually reads. They skim the city page, their thumb stops on a card, and in about two seconds they decide whether to call, tap through or keep scrolling. On a desktop you can get away with a busy card because there is room around it. On a phone the card is the whole screen, and every extra line pushes the next business further down.

Most directory owners design the card once, on a laptop, and never look at it again. This note is about the opposite habit: designing the card for a narrow screen first and letting the wider layouts follow.

Start with the decision the visitor is making

Before you choose fonts or colors, write down what someone needs to know to pick a business from a list. For a plumber directory it might be the name, the town, whether they answer emergency calls and how to reach them. For a bakery directory it is closer to the name, the neighborhood, opening hours and a photo that shows the food.

That short list is your card. Anything that doesn't help the decision belongs on the listing page, one tap away. Owners often try to fit the full address, every category, the website, the rating, a description and three buttons into the card. On a 390 pixel wide screen that turns into a dense block that nobody reads.

A useful test is to cover the card with your hand, show it to someone for two seconds and ask what they remember. If they can tell you the name and one reason to choose it, the card is doing its job.

Give the business name the most weight

The name should be the largest and darkest text on the card. It sounds obvious, but templates often give the category label or the rating the same visual weight, so the eye has nowhere to land first.

Some practical rules that hold up across niches:

  • Keep the name on one or two lines and let long names wrap instead of shrinking the font.
  • Put the town or neighborhood directly under the name in a smaller, lighter style.
  • Use one category label at most, and only when the page mixes categories.
  • Avoid all caps for names, since many business names are already long.

Google Maps data brings its own quirks. Some businesses stuff keywords into their listed name, so a card might read "Best Plumber Springfield 24 Hour Emergency Drain Service". You can't always rewrite names, but you can cap the display length and show the full name on the listing page.

Make the rating honest and easy to read

Ratings help people choose, and they are also the part of a card most likely to mislead. A 5.0 from three reviews and a 4.6 from four hundred mean very different things. Show the review count next to the stars every time, in the same line, so the two numbers are read together.

If you sort a list by rating, say so near the top of the page. If you sort some other way, say that instead. The longer note on showing Google ratings fairly on a directory covers sorting, low review counts and what to do when a business has no rating at all.

On the card itself, keep the rating small. It supports the decision. It shouldn't be the first thing the eye hits, or every card starts to look like a score sheet.

Build tap targets for thumbs

A phone card usually needs one primary action. For service businesses that is a call button. For restaurants and shops it might be directions or a link to the listing page. Pick one and make it obvious.

Two buttons can work if they are clearly different, such as "Call" and "Details". Three or more on a small card leads to mis-taps. Give each button enough height to hit with a thumb and enough space between them that a slightly off tap doesn't dial the wrong number.

Make the whole card tappable as a link to the listing page, with the call button as a separate target inside it. People expect to tap anywhere on a card, and a card that only responds to a small text link feels broken.

Handle missing data without breaking the layout

Scraped data has gaps. Some businesses have no photo, some have no phone number and some have no hours. A card designed around a perfect record will look broken for a third of the listings.

Decide in advance what each card does when a field is empty. A missing photo can fall back to a plain colored block with the business initial, or the card can drop the image area and tighten up. A missing phone number should hide the call button rather than show a dead one. Missing hours should simply not appear. "Hours unavailable" adds a line that tells the visitor nothing.

Test with real exports. Pull twenty random rows from your Google Maps data, including the worst ones, and look at how those cards render on an actual phone. A design that only works with tidy data isn't finished yet.

Check the card in context

A single card can look fine and still fail in a list. Scroll a full city page on a phone and watch for rhythm. Cards of wildly different heights make the page feel jumpy, so consider fixed image heights and limits on description length.

Look at spacing between cards too. Too little and the cards blur together. Too much and a visitor sees one business per screen, which makes a list of forty feel endless. Questions about directory listing cards

Should every listing card have a photo?

Only if most of your listings have usable photos. If half the cards fall back to placeholders, a text-first card often looks cleaner and loads faster.

How much description text fits on a phone card?

One or two short lines at most. Longer descriptions belong on the listing page, where the visitor has already shown interest.

Where should featured listings go?

If you sell featured placements, mark them clearly with a visible label so visitors know the position was paid for, and keep the card design the same so it doesn't look like a different kind of result.
